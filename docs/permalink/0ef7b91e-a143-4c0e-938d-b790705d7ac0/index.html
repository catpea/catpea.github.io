The Lecture Dream

9/26/2026

This is a presentation I dreamed of delivering at the Royal Institution during Christmas Lectures.

Terrestrial life is a profoundly unbroken, historically continuous chemical process whose antecedents extend into prebiotic chemistry.

The emergence of life is a transformation within that unbroken chemical process.

Continuity requires a physically embodied succession in which later organization develops from earlier organization.

Dormancy absolutely, preserves this continuity wherever the capacity for renewed activity survives within or without. Recovery from dormancy continues an existing biological history; it obviously does not begin that history again.

Every surviving lineage, represents a continuing path back through that history with a single origin.

A complete extinction would occur with the loss of all viable continuations.

Any subsequent independent emergence of life would establish a new lineage, a stopped process will not resume, it is an extinguished one.

The start is likely pre-biotic and demands different chemistry from ongoing continuity.

How old are you?

Keep that number in mind. It tells you something about your age as an individual.

But the living history that produced you reaches back billions of years.

Your beginning was not its beginning.

You are not merely descended from primordial life. You are part of its continuation.

We often describe evolution as a succession of organisms.

One generation follows another.

One species gives rise to others.

We draw a tree, put names on its branches, and trace our ancestry backwards.

But look at what those connecting lines represent.

They are not gaps, they represent the transmission and transformation of living organization.

The scientific picture of common descent connects present cellular life through a shared ancestral history.

I want us to see that history not merely as a sequence of things that lived, but as a chemical process that kept producing its own continuation.

An organism begins.

Its process does not, cannot begin again.

Allow me to repeat that: Its process cannot begin again.

Consider what a developing embryo inherits.

Not merely genetic instructions, but molecular equipment already capable of doing work.

In the developing worm, maternally supplied ribosomal machinery is not merely an inheritance preserved from the past. It participates in the protein synthesis that builds the developing animal. The machinery supplied by one generation helps perform the construction of the next.

But notice what that means: the construction is happening around living activity—not between one episode of life and another.

We describe a parent and an offspring, and our language encourages us to imagine two separate productions. First this organism; then that organism.

Look instead at the work.

The offspring is not assembled as an inactive object and brought to life when construction is complete. Its development is accomplished through the operation of an inherited cellular system.

Construction is not something that happens before this life can operate. Construction is something the living system is doing.

This is the process-centred picture: existing molecular activity producing the structures through which further activity becomes possible. Not a completed machine followed by another completed machine, but living machinery participating in the construction of its continuation.

The boundary between generations identifies a new individual. It must not make us imagine a corresponding gap in which the living construction disappears.

The beginning of the offspring is an event within that construction—not the beginning of construction itself.

And those builders participate in making further builders.

Ribosomes manufacture proteins, including protein components needed for new ribosomes. The apparatus that produces proteins also produces components of the apparatus that will continue producing them.

This is chemistry constructing the means of further chemistry.

Think about the unstoppable mechanical audacity of that. Not a machine assembled, completed, and finally switched on.

A system whose operation includes constantly rebuilding its machinery. Whose products become participants in its production. Whose construction is never merely behind it, because construction is part of what it is doing.

Life does not simply have machinery. Life is a process that keeps constructing the machinery of its own continuation.

Now extend that picture across generations.

The extraordinary thing is not that the original components have lasted. It is that later machinery has arisen through the work and organization of earlier machinery.

The material changes. The structures change. The living system furnishes the means by which its successors can develop.

Reproduction, seen this way, is not a series of fresh origins. It is how the continuing process branches.

A new individual is genuinely new. But the living organization from which that individual develops is inherited.

And a branch can wait. In bacterial spores, molecular structures retained through dormancy help initiate recovery when suitable conditions return. The machinery of continuation has survived the interval. Its return to activity is not another origin of life.

That is the full continuity I am asking you to picture: not every component moving at every instant, but the physical means of continuing carried forward rather than lost and independently recreated.

Now look around this room.

We are accustomed to seeing separate people, each with a separate beginning.

We are different places where one primordial chemical process continues as life.

One branching history, taking distinct physical forms.

Our connection is therefore more than resemblance. More than a shared set of chemical ingredients. More than information copied from an ancient source.

We are connected through the continuing physical activity by which life maintains, reconstructs, and extends itself.

You are not looking only at the results of the history of life.

You are looking at that history still happening.

Your body is not simply something the process made.

Your body is somewhere the process continues.
