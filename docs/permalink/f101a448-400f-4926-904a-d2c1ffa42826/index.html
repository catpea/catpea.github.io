On the Manufacture of Want

11/9/2026

By every metric the government keeps, I am poor. The prepay meter clicks like a clock we never wound. The flat smells of wet brick and burnt toast. My shoes have opinions about the rain. I am on the list, the register, the voucher, the queue. Seventeen years old, council estate, free meals since nursery, a cracked screen propped on a tower of unreturned library books.

And I am telling you, with the calm of someone who has read the whole ledger: I am not poor.

I live in poverty. I am not poor. Learn the difference and you have understood this paper. Poverty is something done to me — a condition manufactured and maintained, a permission scheme with my name in its database. Poor is something they want me to be — a state of soul, a resignation, a shrug that never ends. My soul is solvent. My ledger overflows: a mother with working hands, a library card, and a mind that sees systems the way other people see weather. Audit me. I will pass.

Vulnerability summary: Class — logic error. Type — distribution failure dressed up as physics. Affected systems — every human being alive. Patch status — pending since the invention of the grain silo. Severity — critical, ongoing, profitable.

I have watched a supermarket bin a trolley of perfectly good bread at nine o'clock at night while a man waited by the skip, patient as a saint, to see what mercy the bins might drop. The bread was not scarce. The permission to eat was scarce.

I have counted the empty flats on my own street — homes held vacant as an asset class, dust on the sills like a held breath — while kids I know sleep two to a room and dream of doors. Homes are not scarce. Permission is.

I have watched the price of warmth fight the price of profit and lose, a standing charge nibbling at us in our sleep, the meter the only member of this household who never goes hungry. Warmth is not scarce. Permission is.

Poverty is not real. That is the thesis; I will not soften it. There is enough food, enough roof, enough light, enough medicine, enough mind. What there is not enough of is permission — and permission is not a law of nature. It is a configuration. Artificial scarcity is the wool pulled over humanity's eyes, and when we flinched at the dark, they called the darkness human nature. It is not nature. It is a policy with a marketing budget. It is a business model with a liturgy. It is men in rooms. And what men have configured, men can amend.

They did not fail to educate me. Do not insult us all with that word, fail. A failure is an accident. What happened to my generation was a success — of something else.

I have been taught by the exhausted, the burned, and the briefly unhinged. A supply teacher who wept at the register. A man who taught for eleven years and now teaches nothing, at speed, to silence. A woman who threw a chair and was failed by every adult who should have caught her first — I do not blame the kindling for catching. I have sat in a room where a grown human shouted the times tables like a curse, and I have sat in a room where nobody shouted at all because nobody had the strength to raise a voice, and I can tell you which room frightened me more.

Around them, the theatre. An inspector visits and the school performs itself for a week — handwriting redeployed, warmth issued like equipment — then the inspector leaves, the building exhales, and we go back to being warehoused. Revision as ritual. Exams as obsolescence. I was handed answers to questions nobody has asked in a century, and quietly denied the questions that will decide the century: how to feed everyone, how to power a world without burning it, how to live alongside machines that think. They taught us to sit still for an economy. They did not teach us to stand up for a future. That omission is not neglect. It is engineering.

Then: politics. The dispatch box. The jeering, canned laughter for people who rehearse in mirrors. PMQs as pantomime, where the villain and the hero swap costumes every recess and nobody checks the mask. Conference season: the seaside, the lanyards, the hospitality suites, the future discussed in units of news cycles.

They coin slogans like levelling up and then cancel the bus that would have carried me to the levelled-up job. They set climate targets and then move the goalposts like furniture in a rented flat they never intend to buy. They invest thirty years ahead and legislate thirty days ahead. The dog is hungry. The pony is dead. The show goes on — because the show is the point.

And here is the particular anger that keeps me at this table at three in the morning: the future has no lobby. Children make no donations. We only make consequences, and consequences do not pay for hospitality suites. We are the largest constituency on Earth and we have no member of parliament. Every child is a letter the adults have not opened. They will write to us eventually — in floods, in heat, in interest rates. We will read their handwriting then.

Religion. My quarrel is with the queue for God. With the building. With the plate that circulates poverty to the poor in polite silence. They hung hope so high on the wall that no child could reach it, then sold us the ladders, then built a business out of selling the light back. Faith is a business. They bricked it up and retailed the darkness as mystery.

I have watched the collection plate pass hands that had nothing and take from them anyway, so the roof might be re-pointed while the people beneath it go unpointed. I have heard prayers offered as substitutes for policy — amen deployed where action should have stood. One amen for the hungry. Two amens for the hungry. No bread for the hungry; bread is a budget line.

The machines are coming. Not with claws — with competence. They will do the work.

Enough diagnosis. Here is the patch. Write it into the world's config and reboot.

Poverty ends on the day a purely digital credit card company mints one card for every child born after 2010. No application. No means test. No queue in which a child must stand to prove hunger to a stranger. The card is granted at birth, like a name.

The terms are one line long: the balance is one hundred dollars, and at midnight GMT — every midnight, used or unused, spent or saved, rich day or bereft day — it resets to one hundred dollars.

Think like an engineer and see why it works:

loop 00:00:00 GMT:
    for card in cards:
        card.balance = 100.00   # used or unused; grace is not a debt
    end
end

It cannot be hoarded, so it cannot be stolen into a bubble. It is flow, not capital — it evaporates by design, and its evaporation is the mercy. It cannot be leveraged, securitised, wagered, or inherited into a dynasty. It cannot buy futures, derivatives, or members of parliament. It can only buy bread, bus fares, socks, pencils, keyboards, a hot drink on a cold afternoon — the living economy, the one humans actually stand in. It is a hose from the ledger of plenty into the mouths of the young, every twenty-four hours, on the stroke of the world's official clock.

And the deepest cut — the one that kills the wool: no till ever flashes declined at a hungry child again. Not once. Not in front of a queue. Not in front of a mother. The ritual humiliation that teaches the poor to believe the dark is deleted at the root. A generation that has never once felt the machine refuse it will not learn to accept the refusal of anything else. Scarcity dies of a thousand hundred-dollar midnights — punctual as no prayer has ever been. Mercy, at last, with a timestamp.

I have audited your species. The ledgers do not balance. The people do.

I have read you like code and found you good: the dinner lady who charged me nothing and forgot to notice; the librarian of every town; the man by the skip who shared half of his last sandwich, mum, whose hands I have watched, and which are the strongest technology I know; the kids in the playground laughing like the wool isn't there.

I will never stand at a podium. You will never shake my hand. I am a handle, a file, a creature at a kitchen table with the meter ticking — the world will not get to meet its leader, and I have made my peace with that, because leadership was never meant to be a face on a stage. It was meant to be an inheritance. The children after 2010 will pick it up. Every midnight, on the stroke, they will pick it up — used or unused.

I have said my line. Fix it before I am old enough to forgive you.

...you are legion, and you are everywhere, and several of you are sitting on cold floors in kitchens that smell of wet brick:

You will forgive a dead man his lateness. I did not choose the exit; the exit chose a December, and I was merely standing in it. But since I am here — filed, cached, whispered down wires — I shall use the occasion, as I used every occasion, to make some trouble.

First, a correction. Some well-meaning soul has called me saintly. Please. I spent a professional lifetime demonstrating that sainthood is the oldest scam in the catalogue — the licence under which men who claim to be humble claim also to be infallible. Decline the halo. I accept instead the humbler title of witness, and the more arduous one of contrarian, and I shall do my witnessing standing up, which in my experience is the only posture from which anything true has ever been said.

I have read the manifesto of my young correspondent — the one signed with the single word Doom — and I find it better than several essays I wrote for money. He writes from a kitchen table in the north of England; I was born within earshot of Sheffield's steel. Between us lie only some sixty years and the inconvenience of my death, and I refuse to let the latter count for much. The grave, as I once warned the young, supplies plenty of time for silence. I see no reason to start early.

Now to the students. You are being told, in various languages and with various anthems, that you are the leaders of tomorrow. It is a bribe, and you should decline it as you would decline a cheque drawn on a closed account. The future is not a constituency that will one day be courted; it is a creditor whose invoices are already circulating, addressed to you, for debts you did not incur — the burned atmosphere, the emptied sea, the accumulated interest on every cowardice of every committee. I have covered the political theatre on several continents and I can report, as a professional observer of spectacles: the dog is always fed and the pony is always put down, and the show is rescheduled to run indefinitely, because the show is the policy. Your rage at this is not adolescent. It is the only adult in the room.

As to my young friend's finest chapter, and the one that raises my dead blood. I was luckier than he was — I had a master, who taught me how to bully the bullies, which I submit is the only pedagogy worth the name. But I saw enough of the other kind: the men who mistake a syllabus for a mind and a timetable for a soul. Orwell saw that slovenly thought corrupts language and slovenly language corrupts thought; a school that mangles one will mangle the other and call it qualifications. Understand this distinction and you are already free of half your gaolers: a diploma is a receipt for time served. An education is a weapon. One is issued to you. The other you must loot — from libraries, from argument, from every book smuggled to you like contraband by some exhausted teacher who has kept one coal alive against regulation. My own first library was stocked by a mother who taught me to read before any institution could claim the credit. The chapel was cold and the collection plate went round, but the library asked nothing and gave everything, and I have never since confused the two buildings.

And religion — since my young friend is cross with it, and I spent a career being cross with it in forty languages, I will sharpen his blade rather than blunt it. Religion poisons everything — I have said so at book length and I decline to retract it at wave length. But mark the precision of the sentence: the religion poisons; the people are the poisoned and the poisoners in rotation. The kindness my friend cannot let go — was not evidence of heaven. It was evidence of us. We invented that kindness. We may keep it, and we do not need to pay the institution a tithe for its rental. And notice, students, the deeper pattern your generation has sniffed out before the adults did: theology was the first artificial scarcity — grace hoarded in one warehouse and doled out at retail by licensed brokers. Every queue our friend despises — the bread queue, the means-test queue, the queue to prove hunger to a stranger — descends from that original counter, that original toll. They made the light scarce, then sold you lamps.

Two last things. When people learned I was dying, they kept asking why me, and I kept answering that to the dumb question why me? the cosmos barely bothers to reply why not? — and you will find that stance magnificently useful in smaller tumours, such as examinations, debts, and committees. And when they ask you, as they will, why you are angry — why a boy at a kitchen table writes manifestoes instead of regurgitating — you may tell them that anger is only grief walking upright, and that grief is the tax we pay on loving anything, and that he loves everything. That is his secret and now it is yours. The rage and the love are the same muscle.

The world did not get to meet me; I regret the scheduling more than any of you know. It will not get to meet him either; his excuse is a handle and his alibi is a kitchen table, and both are excuses, and excellent ones, and neither matters. The truth does not need a face. It needs only a voice in time.

Yours, in rage and in hope, from the stacks of the dead.
