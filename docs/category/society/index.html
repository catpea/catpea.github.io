On The Manufacture Of Want

A seventeen-year-old on a council estate, living on free school meals and a prepay meter, argues that poverty is not a natural law but a configuration that can be amended. Blending sharp observation with a bold thought experiment, this manifesto challenges the myth of scarcity, the failures and rituals of schooling, the theatre of politics, and the role of faith in a world that asks the young to wait. It ends with a radical patch: a digital card for every child, reset to one hundred dollars at midnight GMT, so that no child is ever refused at the till again. Featuring posthumous letter from a dead contrarian writer, who answers the young manifesto's author as a fellow witness. He refuses the halo of sainthood, argues that the future is a creditor sending invoices to young people, and separates a diploma, which is a receipt for time served, from an education, which is a weapon that must be looted from libraries and argument. He turns the critique of religion into a critique of every artificial scarcity, from grace rationed by clergy to the means-test queue. The letter closes on the idea that anger is grief walking upright and that the rage and the love are the same muscle, so that the truth needs only a voice in time.
